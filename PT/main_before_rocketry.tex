\section{Research plan}\label{sec:research}

My doctoral research focused on understanding the fundamental mechanisms governing turbulent flows under supercritical thermodynamic conditions, where strong variations in thermophysical properties influence flow dynamics and transport processes.
During my doctoral research at the Universitat Politècnica de Catalunya (UPC), I investigated these phenomena through direct numerical simulation (DNS), contributing to the detailed characterization of flow states and turbulence organization in wall-bounded supercritical flows under different thermophysical and buoyancy regimes [J2--J7]\footnote{My own publications are cited with the numbering of Section~3.4 of the \textit{Historial Acadèmic i Professional} (HAP), and listed at the end of the references: [J] articles in indexed journals; [R] submitted articles, proceedings and articles in preparation; [C] conference papers; and [D] datasets.}.
In parallel, I contributed to the development of RHEA-X, a GPU-native, high-fidelity flow solver designed to address the computational demands of multiphysics turbulence [J1].
My most recent research concerned the physical mechanisms underlying extreme thermal events, leading to the development of Lagrangian and information-theoretic (causality assessment) approaches during the 2026 Center for Turbulence Research (CTR) Summer Program at Stanford University [R2, R3].
These contributions establish the scientific and computational foundations for the research program proposed here.
Its central question is whether extreme heat-transfer events and deterioration in supercritical turbulence can be predicted from their physical causes. To answer it, I will combine high-fidelity databases, causal and data-driven analysis, and GPU-accelerated computing.

\subsection{Research context and motivation}\label{sec:background}

Supercritical fluids offer significant opportunities to improve the efficiency, performance, and compactness of advanced energy and propulsion technologies \cite{Li2025,Guardone2024}.
In aerospace propulsion, liquid rocket engines frequently operate at pressures exceeding the critical pressures of their propellants.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:regen}a,b, these are first passed through regenerative cooling channels of thrust chambers and subsequently injected into high-pressure combustors~\cite{Nasuti2021,JofreUrzay2021}.
Computational design and additive manufacturing now produce increasingly complex cooling passages (Figures~\ref{fig:regen}a and~\ref{fig:duct}a)~\cite{LEAP71,Gradl2019}, whose design relies on accurate predictions of heat transfer at supercritical pressure.
High-pressure injection and film cooling further motivate the investigation of mixing and thermal protection under supercritical conditions.
In energy technologies, supercritical CO$_2$ (sCO$_2$) Brayton cycles offer high thermal efficiencies and substantially more compact turbomachinery than conventional steam-based systems, with applications in waste-heat recovery, energy storage, and nuclear and concentrated solar power generation \cite{Li2025,Guardone2024}.
These advantages are closely connected to the distinctive thermophysical properties of fluids operating at supercritical pressures, particularly near the pseudo-boiling region \cite{Banuti2015}.
In this region, pronounced variations in density, viscosity, thermal conductivity, and specific heat (Figure~\ref{fig:regen}c) can substantially modify turbulence, mixing, and heat transfer \cite{Li2025}.
Despite their efficiency advantages, supercritical fluids can exhibit heat-transfer deterioration (HTD) under certain conditions, causing severe localized wall-temperature rises that threaten component reliability (Figure~\ref{fig:regen}b) \cite{Pizzarelli2018,Nasuti2021,Li2025}.

Despite substantial advances in understanding supercritical fluid flows, current knowledge remains largely confined to canonical configurations and restricted operating conditions \cite{Li2025}. High-fidelity simulations have revealed how strong thermophysical-property variations reshape turbulence and thermal transport \cite{Peeters2016,Guo2022}, yet their influence at higher Reynolds numbers, in complex geometries, and under nonuniform heating remains insufficiently understood. 
Square ducts, in particular, provide a canonical configuration for investigating how turbulence anisotropy and secondary motions interact with strong property variations (Figure~\ref{fig:duct}). 
These limitations are critical when predicting HTD, as it remains strongly dependent on operating conditions and flow configuration: even a correlation with Nusselt-number errors below 20\,\% can mispredict the peak wall temperature by up to 200~K near the critical pressure~\cite{Nasuti2021}. 
Likewise, pseudo-boiling can profoundly alter jet stability and development, yet its effects on entrainment and mixing remain poorly understood, limiting our ability to predict and optimize high-pressure injection and film cooling \cite{Lapenna2019,SharanBellan2021}.
Addressing these gaps is essential to exploiting supercritical-fluid properties while ensuring reliable thermal performance.

Beyond mean flow behavior, intermittent turbulent events can generate extreme wall-heat-flux fluctuations and localized thermal loads overlooked by conventional mean-based approaches.
Previous studies have characterized extreme wall events primarily in ideal-gas and constant-property turbulence \cite{Hack2021,Zhang2023}, but their underlying mechanisms and predictive precursors under supercritical conditions remain poorly understood.
In my preliminary investigations of turbulent CO$_2$ channel flow, I identified fluctuations exceeding ten times their root-mean-square value, associated with near-wall vortices transporting fluid of contrasting temperatures towards the wall (Figure~\ref{fig:extreme}a,b) [R3].
As suggested by these findings and my preliminary work at Stanford [R2, R3], information-theoretic causal analysis offers a promising approach to identifying the mechanisms and precursors of extreme events \cite{LozanoDuran2020,Massaro2023}.
Such advances could improve the prediction of critical operating conditions and the reliability of thermal systems.

Moreover, addressing these challenges requires increasingly sophisticated computational capabilities.
High-fidelity simulations must resolve strong property gradients, investigate increasingly demanding flow conditions, and capture sufficient statistics of rare events, imposing substantial computational costs.
These requirements motivate the continued development of efficient, scalable numerical methods capable of exploiting modern GPU-based supercomputing architectures.
Building on RHEA-X [J1], advances in real-fluid property evaluation, computational performance, and Lagrangian analysis will support these investigations.
By integrating fundamental flow physics, causal analysis of extreme events, and high-performance scientific computing, the proposed research program aims to advance the understanding and prediction of supercritical turbulent flows, ultimately contributing to more efficient and reliable energy and propulsion technologies.

\subsubsection{Line 1 -- Physics of supercritical turbulent flows}

My doctoral investigations demonstrated how pseudo-boiling can be exploited to enhance heat transfer and fundamentally modify turbulence in microconfined flows~[J2--J7].
In asymmetrically heated microducts, I showed that pseudo-boiling can trigger turbulence-like regimes under hydrodynamic conditions that remain laminar at low pressure, increasing the hot-wall Nusselt number by approximately $20\times$ with the same imposed temperature difference [J3, J4, J7].
In turbulent square ducts, I demonstrated that secondary-flow intensity can reach 4.6\,\% of the bulk velocity, approximately twice the upper bound reported for canonical ducts.
Moreover, changing the orientation of gravity altered secondary-flow intensity by more than 100\,\% and the hot- and cold-wall Nusselt numbers by up to 28\,\% and 63\,\%, respectively, despite variations below 10\,\% and 3\,\% in bulk and friction Reynolds numbers.
These findings revealed how buoyancy and pseudo-boiling interact to reorganize secondary motions and redistribute momentum, wall shear stress, and turbulent transport [J2, R1, C1].
During my research, I also revisited conventional DNS practices and established refined resolution and minimal-flow-unit guidelines for pseudo-boiling turbulence [J5, J6], demonstrating that grids reproducing first-order statistics within 2\,\% can nevertheless underestimate the intensity and wall proximity of the pseudo-boiling region, with substantial impact on turbulent intensities.

Informed by these contributions, this research line will pursue three complementary investigations, progressing from fundamental transport mechanisms to predictive correlations and the characterization of HTD in increasingly engineering-relevant configurations for space propulsion and thermodynamic cycle technologies:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Thermal transport mechanisms in asymmetrically heated ducts.}
	The first investigation will extend my previous analyses of asymmetrically heated channels and square ducts, where pseudo-boiling interacts with buoyancy and turbulence anisotropy.
	Although the organization of momentum transport and secondary motions has been characterized, the corresponding thermal mechanisms remain less thoroughly understood.
	Using existing DNS databases, I will investigate enthalpy and turbulent heat-flux budgets to quantify how buoyancy, secondary motions, and pseudo-boiling redistribute thermal energy and influence local heat transfer.
	In parallel, attention will be devoted to higher-order statistics and coherent turbulent structures associated with extreme wall-heat-flux events, especially at the heated wall.
	These analyses will establish a comprehensive physical interpretation of heat-transfer modulation and provide reference cases for the causal investigations developed in Line~2.
	
	\item \textbf{Generalized scaling laws and predictive correlations.}
	The second investigation, with simulations already underway (Figure~\ref{fig:duct}b), will examine high-pressure transcritical rectangular ducts with uniform perimeter heating, enabling systematic comparisons with canonical duct flows.
	By varying Reynolds number, thermophysical conditions, and aspect ratio, I will investigate the scaling of skin friction and Nusselt number, alongside momentum and thermal balances.
	Since existing correlations and variable-property transformations may lose accuracy under pseudo-boiling conditions \cite{Modesti2026,Li2025}, these simulations will support their critical assessment and the development of improved physics-based correlations and complementary data-driven models, an effort already initiated in collaboration with Prof.~Xiang Yang (Pennsylvania State University).
	The ambition is to establish broadly applicable predictive relationships that capture the combined influence of Reynolds number, turbulence anisotropy, buoyancy, and thermophysical conditions.
	
	\item \textbf{Heat-transfer deterioration in spatially developing flows.}
	The third investigation will extend the analysis towards spatially developing flows representative of supercritical cooling passages.
	Although previous studies have identified operating conditions associated with HTD, accurately predicting its onset remains challenging, and the underlying physical mechanisms continue to be debated \cite{Nasuti2021,Li2025}.
	I will perform DNS of spatially developing channels and square ducts heated from one side, progressively incorporating more representative heating conditions and cooling-passage geometries.
	These simulations will identify the mechanisms governing HTD onset, including the potentially synergistic influence of buoyancy, secondary motions, and the evolving thermal boundary layer.
	The resulting databases will also provide a demanding assessment of the predictive relationships developed in the second investigation, identifying their limitations and guiding their extension towards deteriorated heat-transfer conditions.
\end{enumerate}
Together, these investigations pursue three complementary scientific objectives:
\begin{itemize}
	\item[\textbf{O1.}] Identify and quantify how pseudo-boiling, buoyancy, and secondary motions govern turbulent heat transfer and extreme wall-heat-flux events in asymmetrically heated ducts relevant to regenerative cooling and energy-conversion systems.
	
	\item[\textbf{O2.}] Establish generalized scaling laws for skin friction and heat transfer in supercritical flows, identifying physically motivated dimensionless parameters that capture the combined effects of Reynolds number and pseudo-boiling to develop improved predictive correlations.
	
	\item[\textbf{O3.}] Elucidate the mechanisms governing HTD onset in spatially developing, asymmetrically heated ducts, quantifying the influence of secondary motions and cooling-passage geometry on thermal deterioration.
\end{itemize}
The program will deliver openly accessible, highly curated DNS databases spanning different heating conditions (both asymmetric and perimeter heating), Reynolds-number effects, and spatially developing flows exhibiting HTD, establishing reference benchmarks for the research community.
These will underpin generalized scaling laws and improved skin-friction and Nusselt-number correlations, including the identification of new dimensionless parameters to potentially capture the combined effects of Reynolds number, turbulence anisotropy in rectangular ducts, buoyancy, and supercritical thermodynamic conditions.
The research will also seek to establish a comprehensive physical understanding of HTD onset, identifying its governing mechanisms and developing predictive criteria for its occurrence.
Ultimately, these contributions will provide a high-fidelity foundation for validating and improving lower-fidelity computational fluid dynamics (CFD) approaches (RANS and LES) and extending predictive capabilities towards increasingly complex, engineering-relevant geometries and operating conditions.
Where feasible, the correlations and HTD criteria developed in this line could also be compared with experiments, potentially through collaborations within GReCEF and the UPC (Section~\ref{sec:mobility}).

Depending on funding availability, a complementary investigation of supercritical jets and mixing layers may also be pursued.
Previous investigations have shown that pseudo-boiling can substantially modify shear-layer instability and jet development \cite{Lapenna2019,SharanBellan2021}, yet its influence on turbulent interfaces and entrainment remains poorly characterized.
Building on the existing capabilities of RHEA-X [J1], I will investigate interactions between liquid-like and gas-like streams of the same fluid, isolating pseudo-boiling effects from the complexities of multicomponent mixing and advancing the understanding of turbulent entrainment relevant to high-pressure injection and film cooling.
In the longer term, this framework could be extended to multispecies mixing and transcritical atomization, addressing the coupled thermodynamic and interfacial phenomena encountered in high-pressure propulsion systems \cite{JofreUrzay2021}.

\subsubsection{Line 2 -- Causality and extreme events in multiphysics turbulence}

Extreme events in turbulent flows, from severe storms and floods to localized thermal loads in engineering systems, often have consequences far beyond those suggested by average conditions.
Rare fluctuations can dominate critical transport processes and impose loads that conventional mean and root-mean-square statistics fail to capture~\cite{Guerrero2020}.
Understanding the mechanisms preceding such events and whether they can be anticipated is therefore essential for improving prediction and reliability.
Conventional approaches such as conditional averaging characterize the turbulent structures around extreme events but do not establish their causal relationships \cite{Hack2021,Zhang2023}.
During the 2026 CTR Summer Program, I initiated an alternative approach combining event-conditioned Eulerian statistics, Lagrangian tracking, and transfer entropy to investigate extreme wall-heat-flux events in sCO$_2$ turbulence [R2, R3].
These investigations revealed a sequence of near-wall turbulent motions preceding extreme thermal events and identified candidate precursors carrying predictive information.
Building on these findings, this research line will develop through two complementary activities:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Causality and prediction of extreme events in supercritical turbulence.}
	This investigation (under development) combines the formalization of a non-stationary Lagrangian information-theoretic framework with its application to extreme wall-heat-flux events.
	Extending the preliminary transfer-entropy analyses [R2, R3], the primary approach to be tested will be the incorporation of multivariate decompositions of unique, redundant, and synergistic contributions \cite{MartinezSanchez2024}.
	Its application to the existing databases will establish how pseudo-boiling influences extreme thermal events and their precursors.
	This work should have reached a mature stage by the start of the Lector appointment to subsequently be applied to the developing-flow databases of Line~1 to identify the causal precursors of HTD and establish predictive criteria for its onset.
	
	\item \textbf{Generalization of the causal analysis approach to multiphysics turbulent systems.}
	The framework will be applied to physically distinct problems.
	The first application will address microplastic--biogenic particle collisions and aggregation in ocean turbulence, drawing on my summer 2027 research stay with Prof.~Mona Rahmani at the University of British Columbia.
	A subsequent research stay at the Universit\`a del Salento with Prof.~Mario Di Renzo and Prof.~Donatella Passiatore will support its extension to extreme thermal events in hypersonic boundary layers.
	A prospective third international stay at the California Institute of Technology, hosted by Prof.~Adrián Lozano-Durán, will provide additional opportunities to consolidate and validate the methodology across turbulent flow regimes (see Section~\ref{sec:stays}).
\end{enumerate}
The natural follow-up scientific objectives are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item[\textbf{O4.}] Develop and validate a non-stationary Lagrangian information-theoretic framework for assessing causal relationships and identifying predictive precursors of rare turbulent events.
	\item[\textbf{O5.}] Identify the causal mechanisms and precursors of extreme events in supercritical turbulence and evaluate their predictive value across thermophysical and buoyancy regimes.
	\item[\textbf{O6.}] Demonstrate the framework's generality through applications to particle interactions in ocean turbulence and extreme thermal events in compressible and hypersonic flows.
\end{itemize}
The expected deliverables include an open-source, validated Lagrangian causality framework, benchmark cases for rare-event analysis, and physically interpretable predictive indicators of extreme wall-heat-flux events and HTD onset.
Its testing across different multiphysics turbulent flows will establish a high-impact transferable methodology for investigating and anticipating extreme events.

\subsubsection{Line 3 -- High-performance computing for high-fidelity simulation of complex turbulent flows}

RHEA-X [J1] is the computational code for the simulations in Lines~1 and~2. 
Improving it is a natural next step as these investigations move towards larger and more complex simulations and research goals. 
Additionally, this line of work serves a broader goal of establishing RHEA-X as a reference tool for high-fidelity CFD by improving its performance while maintaining reproducibility, and enabling researchers to add physical models and diagnostics through documented interfaces that reuse optimized GPU routines.
I co-developed RHEA-X, the successor to the open-source RHEA solver~\cite{RHEASoftware}, and led its port to distributed GPUs. 
The port retains a common C++ source for CPU and GPU (OpenACC~\cite{OpenACC}) execution, keeps flow fields resident on the device, and uses GPU-aware MPI for halo exchanges. 
Benchmarks on different supercomputers (such as MareNostrum~5 at the Barcelona Supercomputing Center (BSC)~\cite{BSC} and the Netherlands' national supercomputer Snellius~\cite{Snellius}) demonstrated speedups of up to $70\times$ over CPU execution and strong-scaling efficiencies of up to $97\,\%$ on as many as 64 GPUs [J1]. 
Extending this foundation, I plan to advance the following topics:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Systematic kernel optimization across CPUs and GPUs.}
	I will benchmark different optimization strategies such as optimized data layout, cache reuse, kernel fusion and splitting, and asynchronous computation on representative DNS workloads. 
	Comparisons across different architectures and paradigms (such as SYCL~\cite{SYCL}) will establish which optimizations transfer across architectures and which depend on the hardware or workload. 
	I will document their effects on runtime, memory use, scaling and agreement in flow statistics, with reproducible cases that other CFD developers can use to test the same choices.
	
	\item \textbf{Reliable substitution of costly physical models with surrogates.}
	I will establish a procedure to determine when a surrogate can replace a computationally demanding model. 
	The first demonstration builds on [R4], where I am contributing to a surrogate model trained on CoolProp data~\cite{CoolProp2014} to replace selected property evaluations based on the Peng--Robinson equation of state~\cite{PengRobinson1976}. 
	Additionally, I will quantify the gains in runtime and device memory alongside the errors in thermophysical properties and their gradients near pseudo-boiling.
	
	\item \textbf{Guidelines for integrating new physical models and diagnostics.}
	I plan to develop documented hybrid C++-OpenACC interfaces, implementation examples and verification cases that let contributors reuse the solver's accelerated capabilities when testing their own ``plug-ins''. 
	The guidelines will show how to implement a module, verify its results on CPUs and GPUs, and measure its cost in a full DNS. 
\end{enumerate}
These contributions aim to establish the code as a general, reproducible platform for high-fidelity CFD in academia and research. Researchers will be able to implement additional physical models straightforwardly, within a unified framework that exploits GPU-accelerated architectures from laptops to supercomputers.
The optimization benchmarks will guide implementation choices, the surrogate-model approach will establish cheaper models leveraging highly reliable databases, and the integration guidelines will make both accessible to potential contributors. 
This should also support longer-term investigations of high-speed supercritical flows, including shock interactions and multicomponent mixing, with reacting flows and finite-rate chemistry as subsequent extensions.
Therefore, two main objectives are established in this research line:
\begin{itemize}
	\item[\textbf{O7.}] Establish reproducible methods for optimizing DNS kernels across accelerated HPC architectures and for validating GPU-friendly surrogate models.
	\item[\textbf{O8.}] Enable researchers to seamlessly integrate and verify GPU-capable models through documented interfaces, promoting scientific collaboration.
\end{itemize}

The deliverables will be versioned open-source releases of RHEA-X, reproducible benchmarks, and implementation guidelines with verified examples. 
These resources will support simulations in Lines~1 and~2, and the generation of documented datasets for physical analysis and data-driven modeling. 
The feasibility of this line also builds on six competitive supercomputing allocations to which I contributed (described in the HAP), two MareNostrum hackathons, ongoing collaboration with Xavier Álvarez-Farré (SURF), and technical exchanges with Lucas Gasparino (BSC) and Filippo Spiga (NVIDIA). 
The shared code and benchmarks will also support joint development and validation during the planned international research stays (Section~\ref{sec:stays}).
Besides participating in further hackathons and continuing to apply for national allocations through the Spanish Supercomputing Network (RES), I plan to leverage this experience to apply for EuroHPC allocations, with the aim of scaling RHEA-X towards exascale performance.
Quantum computing is also a longer-term interest, in particular its feasibility for CFD, even as a partial offload~\cite{Bharadwaj2023}.

\subsubsection{Long-term direction}

By the end of the contract, and looking further ahead, the program aims to predict the consequences of strong property variations in engineering flows: both the transport and mixing enhancement that the applications seek and the HTD and extreme thermal loads that limit them, in regenerative cooling channels and supercritical heat exchangers~\cite{Pizzarelli2018,Ahn2015}. 
The same capabilities lead naturally to high-speed aerothermodynamics, where extreme wall heat fluxes, large property variations and thermochemical effects coexist~\cite{Urzay2018,DiRenzoUrzay2021,Passiatore2022}. 
This extension continues my work on variable-property turbulence, extreme thermal events and GPU-accelerated high-fidelity CFD. 
It starts with T2.3 at the Universit\`a del Salento, whose hosts have studied hypersonic boundary layers~\cite{DiRenzoUrzay2021,Passiatore2022}, is consolidated during the Caltech stay, and is in line with the department's priority on compressible flows. 
Extending RHEA-X to high-speed supercritical flows, with shock interactions and multicomponent mixing, is a longer-term development (Line~3).
Therefore, the ERC Starting Grant is placed deliberately at the end of the contract. 
By then, the proposal can build on the results of the first independent project, experience managing competitive funding, doctoral supervision, an independent publication record, and the collaborations established at UBC, Salento and Caltech. 
Its scientific question will go substantially beyond the national project: from understanding supercritical turbulence and extreme events to their causal prediction in real-fluid and high-speed flows.
The proposal would also include an experimental component, to test whether the precursors and criteria identified through simulations can anticipate HTD through measurable quantities, such as wall temperature.

\subsubsection{Engineering transfer} 
From Phase~2 onwards, the program should carry its results to engineering design in two steps.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The DNS databases would calibrate and validate the models that engineering tools need. In particular, correlations and transformations (O2) would be tuned, as well as turbulent Prandtl number and wall models for RANS and LES, and data-driven models of velocity and temperature profiles. The real-fluid property surrogate models or other developed methods in RHEA-X of Line~3 [R4] could potentially be coupled to OpenFOAM, so that RANS and LES of real cooling passages and heat exchangers use the thermophysics validated by DNS.
	
	\item These tools will be applied to real industrial applications such as regeneratively cooled channels produced by additive manufacturing, including wall roughness and conjugate heat transfer (Figures~\ref{fig:regen}a and~\ref{fig:duct}a); 
	sCO$_2$ Brayton cycles, whose precoolers, recuperators and compressor inlet operate near the pseudo-critical point~\cite{Ahn2015}; 
	transcritical CO$_2$ heat pumps, whose gas coolers operate across pseudo-boiling; and supercritical organic Rankine cycles for waste-heat recovery. 
\end{enumerate}
Waste heat accounts for more than half of the global primary energy~\cite{Markides2025}, and the working fluids of these cycles are changing, highlighting the need for research and development in such technologies. The EU F-gas Regulation phases out HFCs by 2050~\cite{FGas2024}, and the proposed EU restriction of PFAS also covers most HFO working fluids~\cite{ECHA2025}. 
Additionally, drawing from Line~2, design margins that account for extreme events could be established, through probabilistic limits on the wall temperature and precursor-based monitoring.
The majority of these activities could also be carried out through industrial doctorates and contracts with companies in the energy and space sectors.

\subsection{Personal vision of teaching}\label{sec:vision}

I see engineering teaching as developing critical thinking and a systematic method to analyze any system: deciding what matters, writing the balance that governs it, anticipating the expected order of magnitude, and checking that the result makes physical sense. 
Students who learn this way solve new problems by reasoning rather than by memorizing, which was the most useful skill I acquired in my own bachelor's and master's studies. 
Thermodynamics, heat transfer and fluid mechanics are well suited to this approach, because most concepts can usually be tied to a device the students may know, may understand through visual representation or may deduce from the typical functioning of devices in their everyday lives, such as heat pumps, a pumping installation, a car engine or a power cycle.

The following four principles have guided my teaching so far:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Moving from simple practical examples to more abstract concepts.} 
  I prefer to start each topic or concept from a simple but real engineering case and only then generalize to the theory and to more complex configurations.
  This progression is also supported by research on concreteness fading in science and mathematics
  instruction~\cite{Fyfe2014}. 
  In TTC, for instance, the cooling of an automotive inverter, drawn from my industrial experience, introduces conduction and convection before thermal resistances, multidimensional geometries and more complex boundary conditions. 
  My research on supercritical fluids and high-performance computing provides further cases at the
  frontier of the field.
  
  \item \textbf{Clarity and closeness.} 
  I prefer to develop the theory step by step on the blackboard, so that derivations unfold at a pace that students can follow while taking their own notes, which I have found keeps them engaged and focused. 
  A few projected auxiliary slides may complement the board with figures and examples of real systems that are difficult to draw, although they are not the main teaching instrument.
  This is also supported by previous surveys. In a seven-year data collection of more than 2,000 physiology students, 87\,\% preferred lectures written on the board, citing a more appropriate pace, easier note-taking and greater attention~\cite{Armour2016}. Another teaching strategy I particularly value is ensuring that students understand the underlying rationale and each step of the reasoning before moving forward. Although classes must maintain a certain pace, I have found that investing additional time in consolidating fundamental concepts helps students build the confidence needed to engage with more complex problems, even when these must subsequently be addressed at a slightly faster pace. This approach encourages students to participate more actively, ask questions, and engage in discussions both during and after class. It also reduces the need to revisit fundamental concepts independently, avoiding the frustration that can arise when students encounter more advanced problems without a solid foundation. While seemingly intuitive, I believe this aspect of teaching is sometimes overlooked in the effort to adhere strictly to the planned syllabus. In my experience, prioritizing depth of understanding over rigid adherence to the schedule, particularly when introducing fundamental concepts, ultimately leads to a more effective and engaging learning process.
  I also keep an approachable relationship with students, who in my experience respond well to suggestions when
  they feel that the lecturer is accessible.
  
  \item \textbf{Formative feedback and project-based learning.}
  Another aspect I particularly value is helping students learn how to conduct technical work systematically and communicate their methods and results effectively, especially through laboratory reports and projects at the undergraduate level. 
  Although the growing use of generative AI has raised concerns about students' independent contributions and critical thinking, particularly when elaborating reports and developing projects, I believe these activities remain essential. 
  This is because preparing technical reports and presenting results are among the tasks engineering graduates will regularly face in both industry and academia. 
  Rather than moving away from these forms of assessment, I believe we should adapt them to incorporate AI in a controlled and purposeful way, teaching students how to use it to support their learning and improve their work without replacing their own reasoning (Section~\ref{sec:ai}).
  In my experience, undergraduate students often struggle not only with analyzing results, but also with structuring their arguments and communicating their findings clearly through tables, diagrams, charts and schematics. I see this as an important opportunity for improvement, particularly because these skills are transferable well beyond the classroom.
  So far, the approach I have found most effective is to provide brief guidelines before the first laboratory report, followed by more detailed and constructive feedback afterward. 
  I make a particular effort to explain not only what could be improved, but also why it matters, relating these improvements to the work they will eventually carry out as engineers or researchers. 
  Students have explicitly appreciated this feedback in teaching surveys, which encourages me to continue developing this approach. 
  The same principle also guides how I conduct exam reviews and clarification sessions in TTC, where I try to turn mistakes and questions into opportunities for learning rather than simply explaining the correct answers.
  
  \item \textbf{Active learning.}
  Given the demonstrated benefits of active learning in science and engineering education~\cite{Freeman2014}, I would like to explore how to incorporate it more systematically into my teaching. 
  In MF laboratory sessions, we already conduct short tests before each practical activity, which provide a useful starting point but whose pedagogical potential I believe could be better exploited. 
  I would like to explore ways of making these activities more interactive and directly connected to the experiments, for instance by encouraging students to formulate predictions and subsequently compare them with their measurements. 
  I am also particularly interested in extending active-learning strategies to theory classes through short conceptual discussions and collaborative, round-table problem-solving activities. 
  My intention is to introduce and test these approaches gradually, evaluate their effectiveness through student participation and feedback, and refine them in coordination with the other lecturers involved in each subject.

\end{itemize}

Following a common classification of engineering studies, the subjects of the call play different roles.
The \emph{core subjects}, such as MF and TTC, set the basis that later subjects assume, while \emph{specialized subjects}, such as \textit{Ingenier\'ia de Fluidos}, \textit{Ingenier\'ia T\'ermica} (Thermal Engineering) and \textit{Mec\'anica de Fluidos Computacional} (Computational Fluid Mechanics), extend this basis to engineering applications. 
In my view, and building on these principles, core subjects should prioritize solid fundamentals and working habits while specialized subjects should connect them to design decisions and to the tools used in industry and research.

\subsection{Developed subjects}\label{sec:dev-approach}

The following sections present the proposed teaching programmes for \textit{Termodinámica y Transferencia de Calor} and \textit{Mecánica de Fluidos Computacional}, corresponding to bachelor's and master's studies, respectively. 
Their design reflects my perspective on the progression of engineering competencies across these two educational levels.
At the bachelor's level, the primary objective should be to establish a rigorous understanding of fundamental physical principles and develop the ability to apply them systematically to real engineering and scientific problems.

Particular attention should be devoted to the verification and validation of results, the assumptions and applicability limits of physical and numerical models, and the trade-offs between accuracy, reliability, and computational cost.

\paragraph{Learning objectives}

In line with the course guide, the course aims to give students basic knowledge of the main thermodynamic principles, the analysis of thermodynamic systems, both power and refrigeration systems, and of the basic heat transfer mechanisms. 
It also develops the understanding of applied thermodynamics and heat transfer and their application to engineering problems.
By the end of the course, students should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item state and apply the zeroth, first and second laws of thermodynamics to closed and   open systems;
  \item model the components of systems involved in thermodynamic cycles (piston--cylinder devices, turbines, compressors, heat exchangers), represent processes in thermodynamic diagrams and evaluate the properties of real substances;
  \item analyze gas, steam, combined and refrigeration cycles, and compute their efficiency;
  \item distinguish conduction, convection and radiation, and apply them to insulation, cooling and heat-exchange problems, and apply the concept of thermal resistance to combined configurations.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Teaching strategy and assessment}\label{sec:ttc-assessment}

In line with the course guide, I propose to combine lectures with active methodologies, individual problem solving and teamwork in the laboratory. 
I would develop the theory on the blackboard, starting each topic, whenever possible, from a practical example or a device that students can recognize (Section~\ref{sec:vision}) while building solid fundamental knowledge, and alternate it with problems solved in class. 
The laboratory sessions (thermometry, calorimetry, conduction, processes and cycles, radiation) would be carried out in teams. 
Course materials would be organized in the Atenea virtual campus with the tools learned in the ICE training, and the problem collections and exams prepared with Julia-based tools.

The assessment scheme of the course guide comprises an in-person mid-term exam (35\,\%), a final exam
(45\,\%), laboratory practices (15\,\%), graded through a pre-test before each session, which encourages students to prepare for it, the laboratory work and the reports, and generic skills (5\,\%), with compulsory attendance at all laboratory sessions and a re-assessment test according to the EEBE regulations. Possible changes to this scheme, such as oral mid-term examinations, are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:ttc-innovation}.
The mid-term exam would cover units 1, 2 and 4, so that students receive feedback on both blocks halfway through the course. 
Within the generic-skills component, I propose short individual tasks based on the notebooks proposed in Section~\ref{sec:ttc-innovation}, which assess self-directed learning and give early feedback before the mid-term exam.

\paragraph{Potential innovation} \label{sec:ttc-innovation}

Within the framework of the course guide and in agreement with the coordinators, I plan to introduce the following elements, first in my own groups:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Computational notebooks.} 
  Short Python notebooks with the open-source library CoolProp~\cite{CoolProp2014} (or NIST's MiniREFPROP) to evaluate properties, build diagrams and explore cycles, so that students can check their hand calculations and see the effect of each parameter. 
  The notebooks could also include visual simulations that students can explore by changing their parameters, such as the evolution of the temperature field in a wall or a composite medium, which exercises the heat conduction equation and its different types of boundary conditions, or the response of a cycle to its operating conditions, to make the fundamentals more engaging. 
  The notebooks would offer bachelor's students a first contact with computational methods and a gateway to MFC (Section~\ref{sec:cfm}).
  \item \textbf{Engineering cases.} Some units can close with a more complex case drawn from industry or research, such as the cooling of an automotive inverter (conduction and convection), the effect of the pseudo-boiling regime on heat transfer (convection), aircraft gas turbines or the regenerative cooling of liquid-rocket engines. These cases also help identify students interested in these topics for final theses (Section~\ref{sec:theses}).
  \item \textbf{Round-table problem sessions.}
  Dedicated sessions within the large-group hours will be devoted to problems that students first discuss in small round-table groups and then solve in class with my support, preceded by short preparatory material, as a first step towards active learning~\cite{Freeman2014} and a flipped classroom (Section~\ref{sec:vision}).
  \item \textbf{Short presentations in the laboratory.}
  At the end of each laboratory session, after a preliminary processing of their results, selected teams present them in about ten minutes, followed by a short discussion, rotating so that every team presents during the course. 
  This trains students to interpret and communicate results while the experiment is still fresh, and can be part of the laboratory grade.
  \item \textbf{Intermediate oral examinations.}
  Short individual oral examinations could replace the written mid-term exam, assessing the understanding and reasoning behind a solution rather than only its result, in line with the oral defenses of Section~\ref{sec:ai}. 
\end{itemize}
The measures on generative AI in TTC are described in Section~\ref{sec:ai}.

\subsection{Teaching innovation and training plan}\label{sec:innovation}

Fluid mechanics and thermodynamics courses have a reputation among students as some of the most demanding of their degrees, which can make them frustrating and less engaging.
I therefore plan to address this perception at different levels, focusing on students' curiosity and motivation.
A first step is to gradually revisit teaching and assessment methods, including the use of generative artificial intelligence (Section~\ref{sec:ai}).
I also plan to connect students with research and industry in related fields, to stimulate their curiosity and interest and, in the long term, to contribute new courses in the areas of expertise that I bring to the department.

\subsubsection{Generative artificial intelligence in learning}\label{sec:ai}

Generative AI is already part of how students study. 
In a survey of nearly 6,000 students at Swedish universities, most reported using ChatGPT, with engineering students showing the highest adoption~\cite{Stohr2024}. 
Its implications for learning, however, depend strongly on how it is used. 
In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 students, unrestricted access to GPT-4 improved performance during practice but reduced subsequent exam performance, whereas an AI tutor providing hints rather than solutions avoided this effect~\cite{Bastani2025}. 
Conversely, a randomized trial in introductory physics found greater learning gains with a pedagogically designed AI tutor than with an in-class active-learning session~\cite{Kestin2025}. 
These findings suggest that AI can support learning when it encourages reasoning, but may undermine it when it replaces students' own efforts.
I believe universities should therefore adapt to this reality rather than simply prohibit these tools, as also advocated by the European University Association~\cite{EUA2023}.
At the UPC, the guide on the use of AI, approved by the Governing Council in July 2026, sets a common framework for its responsible use and excludes automated decisions that affect assessment without human supervision~\cite{UPCAI2026}, while the ICE offers AI training for teaching staff~\cite{UPCAITeach}, which I intend to pursue.
Building on my teaching philosophy, I would like to explore adaptations that preserve independent reasoning while allowing students to develop the skills needed to use modern tools effectively. 
In coordination with the subject coordinators, I would particularly consider:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Clear AI-use guidelines.} Specify whether AI is prohibited, permitted with disclosure, or encouraged in each activity. Supervised examinations and tests would remain AI-free, ensuring that individual understanding continues to be assessed independently.
	
	\item \textbf{Greater emphasis on supervised technical work.} Explore shifting a larger share of laboratory and project assessment towards in-class calculations, analysis, presentation of results, and short individual oral defenses. Final technical reports would remain important professional deliverables, but with a smaller assessment weight and permitted AI assistance for improving their structure, clarity, and presentation.
	
	\item \textbf{AI-assisted independent learning.} Explore, in the longer term, a course-specific assistant based on UPC-approved tools and teaching materials, designed to guide students through questions and hints rather than provide complete solutions, complementing classroom explanations and individual practice.
\end{itemize}

I would introduce these measures gradually, initially through selected activities and in agreement with the teaching teams. Their effectiveness could be evaluated through independent assessments, student feedback, and, where appropriate, comparisons between parallel TTC groups. The most promising approaches could subsequently be refined and extended, potentially contributing to a teaching-innovation project through the ICE.
