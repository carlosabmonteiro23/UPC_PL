self-assessment at the end of year 3 that matches the evaluation foreseen in article 78

methods and link final theses to the research plan.

\section{Research plan}

\subsection{Research background}
My PhD at the UPC (2023--2026, Excellent \emph{cum laude}) studied \emph{pseudo-boiling
wall-bounded turbulence in supercritical flows}. Using direct numerical simulation with
real-gas thermodynamics, I characterised the flow regimes of high-pressure transcritical
fluids in microducts [J4]. I also contributed to an open database of laminar, transitional
and turbulent microconfined channel flows [J3, D2] and helped analyse the matching
experiments with data-driven methods [J7]. To make these simulations reliable and
affordable, I established resolution standards for DNS of transcritical wall turbulence
[J5] and studied minimal flow units [J6]. Most recently I explained how buoyancy drives
secondary flows and redistributes momentum in transcritical duct turbulence [J2]
(\emph{Physics of Fluids} \emph{Editor's Pick}). This work forms the basis of \textbf{line A}
(multiphysics, compressible and supercritical turbulence).

All of these simulations rely on my work in \textbf{line C}. I have led the development of
RHEA-X, the distributed, GPU-resident version of the open-source solver RHEA
(MPI + OpenACC/CUDA) [J1]. I optimised it at the BSC MareNostrum Hackathons and have used it
in competitive allocations at MareNostrum~5, Finisterrae~III and Canadian national
systems (more than 10 million core/GPU hours in total).

In 2026 I opened \textbf{line B} at the CTR Summer Program at Stanford University, with
the project \emph{Hunting Thermofluid-Driven Transcritical Black Swans: Lagrangian Intermittency in Supercritical Fluid Turbulence}. The RES allocation IM-2026-1-0034 and the BLASTNet machine-learning dataset [D1] extend it. I will
continue this work at the University of British Columbia in summer 2027 (Mitacs Globalink
Research Award) and with the group of Prof.\ Mario Di Renzo in 2028. This plan builds
directly on these three foundations. (References [J*], [C*], [D*] follow the numbering of
the HAP; numbered references are listed at the end of this document.)

\subsection{Motivation and research objectives}
Supercritical and transcritical fluids are central to several technologies of the
energy transition: supercritical CO$_2$ power cycles~\cite{Ahn2015}, liquid-rocket and
scramjet cooling, high-pressure propulsion and injection~\cite{JofreUrzay2021}, and
supercritical water-cooled reactors~\cite{Yoo2013}.
Near the pseudo-critical line, thermophysical properties change by orders of magnitude
over very small temperature intervals. The resulting turbulence is strongly coupled with
thermodynamics and heat transfer. Rare, intense events in that turbulence, such as local
heat-transfer deterioration, pressure peaks or flow reversal, govern safety margins and
design limits~\cite{Sapsis2021}. Yet their causes are poorly understood and they are hard to predict.
High-fidelity simulations can resolve these flows, but only on large GPU-based
supercomputers, and the data they produce require new analysis tools.

This plan addresses the problem along the three lines of the call:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.6cm]
\item[Line A -- Multiphysics turbulent flows; compressible and supercritical fluids.]\mbox{}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item[O1.] Generate high-fidelity (DNS/wall-resolved LES) databases of wall-bounded and
  free-shear turbulence at supercritical pressure, with real-gas thermodynamics and
  transport properties. They will cover heated and cooled channels and pipes, as well as
  transcritical mixing layers and jets.
  \item[O2.] Characterise the multiphysics coupling between turbulence, heat transfer and
  thermodynamic non-idealities (pseudo-boiling, compressibility, buoyancy), and its effect
  on heat-transfer deterioration and enhancement.
  \end{itemize}
\item[Line B -- Data-driven prediction of engineering flows; extreme events; causality.]\mbox{}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item[O3.] Identify the causal mechanisms that drive extreme events (e.g.\ peaks of
  wall heat flux and wall shear stress) using information-theoretic causality
  \cite{LozanoDuranArranz2022,MartinezSanchez2024} and conditional statistics. This will build on the work started at CTR-Stanford in 2026 and
  continue at UBC in 2027.
  \item[O4.] Develop data-driven, causality-informed models that predict these extreme
  events and other engineering quantities of interest: precursors, reduced-order models and
  neural-network surrogates~\cite{Brunton2020}.
  \end{itemize}
\item[Line C -- Modelling and computation; distributed (CPU) and accelerated (GPU) programming.]\mbox{}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item[O5.] Develop and maintain performance-portable, open-source solvers for
  compressible real-gas flows on hybrid CPU--GPU supercomputers (MPI + OpenACC/CUDA), with
  in-situ data analysis and machine-learning coupling. This will build on RHEA-X [J1],
  whose development I lead.
  \item[O6.] Develop subgrid-scale and wall models for supercritical turbulence that are
  consistent with real-gas thermodynamics, trained and validated on the O1 databases.
  \end{itemize}
\end{description}

\subsection{Personal vision of teaching}
I see engineering teaching as training students to \emph{model} a real system: to decide
what matters, write the balance that governs it, estimate the answer before computing it
and check that the result makes physical sense. Thermodynamics, heat transfer and fluid
mechanics are ideal for this, because every concept can be tied to a device the students
know: a heat pump, a pump installation, a car's aerodynamics, a power cycle.

Three principles guide my teaching. \textbf{Clarity and closeness}: in the laboratories I
explain each session step by step and make sure everyone has understood before moving on,
which students value in the surveys (4.6--4.8 out of 5). \textbf{Feedback to
learn, not only to grade}: I return detailed comments on every report and exam so that
students improve progressively; the same idea guides the exam reviews in TTC.
\textbf{Bringing current engineering into the classroom}: my industrial experience
(thermal simulation at Continental, aerodynamics in Formula Student) and my research on
supercritical fluids and high-performance computing provide examples and computational
tools that make the classical content relevant. Active learning has been shown to improve
student performance in science and engineering~\cite{Freeman2014}, and I will introduce it
progressively, coordinated with the other lecturers of each subject.

\subsection{Developed subject: Thermodynamics and Heat Transfer (TTC)}\label{sec:ttc}
% \NOTE{Adapted from Section 4.5 of the Professor Agregat project; rewrite in your own words and check against the current course guide of TTC.}

\textbf{Prerequisites.} Physics II and Numerical Calculus--Differential Equations passed;
Fluid Mechanics taken in parallel or before.

\textbf{Learning objectives.} By the end of the course, students should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item state and apply the zeroth, first and second laws of thermodynamics to closed and open systems;
\item model components (piston--cylinder, turbines, compressors, heat exchangers), represent
processes in thermodynamic diagrams and evaluate properties of real substances;
\item analyse power and refrigeration cycles (Rankine, Brayton, vapour compression) and compute their efficiency;
\item distinguish conduction, convection and radiation, and apply them to insulation, cooling and heat-exchange problems.
\end{itemize}
Transversal competence: autonomous learning (level 2).

\textbf{Syllabus.}
(1) Fundamentals: systems, temperature, ideal gas, pure-substance properties.
(2) First law: work, internal energy, heat, enthalpy, specific heats, polytropic processes, closed and open systems.
(3) Second law: entropy, irreversibilities, heat engines, Carnot cycle, isentropic efficiencies, Brayton, Rankine and refrigeration cycles.
(4) Conduction: heat equation, plane and cylindrical walls, thermal resistances, overall coefficient.
(5) Convection: natural and forced, internal and external flows, empirical correlations.
(6) Radiation: black and grey bodies, Kirchhoff's law, view factors, radiative exchange.

\textbf{Methodology.} Participative lectures are combined with a \emph{flipped
classroom}. Each week a one-hour session introduces the next block and the material to
study (short videos, notes, solved examples). The two-hour session is spent on problem
solving in small groups. Students use computational property tools (e.g.\ the
open-source library CoolProp~\cite{CoolProp2014} in Python notebooks) to explore real-fluid behaviour and
cycles. A mini-project links the course to current technology, e.g.\ a supercritical
CO$_2$ cycle.

\subsection{Fluid Engineering}\label{sec:ef}
From spring 2027 I will teach two theory groups of \emph{Enginyeria de Fluids} (340058,
6 ECTS) at the EPSEVG, a compulsory subject of the Bachelor's degree in Mechanical
Engineering (optional in Electrical and in Industrial Electronics and Automatic Control
Engineering). I will teach theory groups M65 and M66, each with two 2-hour sessions per week
(one every week and one in A and B1 weeks only), i.e.\ about 92 contact hours over the semester
(15/02--04/06/2027).

\textbf{Prerequisites.} Differential equations, advanced calculus and \emph{Fluid
Mechanics} (340039). Specific competence CE24: applied knowledge of the fundamentals of
fluid-mechanical systems and machines.

\textbf{Learning objectives.} By the end of the course, students should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item apply the differential equations of fluid motion and dimensional analysis and
similarity to engineering problems and model testing;
\item compute drag and lift on bodies in external flow for simple geometries;
\item describe the fundamentals of turbomachines and analyse hydraulic installations with
pumps, including operating point, variable speed, pump selection and cavitation;
\item use specific software to simulate hydraulic networks;
\item solve one-dimensional compressible flows in nozzles and ducts;
\item work in a team, taking part in its coordination.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Syllabus} (official course guide).
(1) Differential analysis of flow: kinematics, continuity, Euler and Navier--Stokes equations.
(2) Dimensional analysis and similarity: Buckingham $\Pi$ theorem, complete and incomplete similarity.
(3) Drag and lift in external flow: boundary layer, friction and pressure drag, cylinders and spheres, lift.
(4) Turbomachines: velocity triangles, Euler's equation, degree of reaction, similarity, characteristic curves.
(5) Installations with turbomachines: system and pump curves, operating point, variable speed, pump selection, cavitation.
(6) Compressible flow: speed of sound, Mach number, stagnation conditions, nozzles and diffusers, normal shocks, Fanno and Rayleigh flows.

\textbf{Methodology.} Theory sessions combine the exposition of the fundamentals with
worked problems; students then solve the problem collections in and out of class. I will
add three elements, consistent with the course guide:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Real-installation case studies} in units 4--5: from the pump curve in a
manufacturer's catalogue to the operating point of a building or irrigation network, which
students then reproduce in the EPANET laboratory session.
\item \emph{Short computational examples} (Python or Julia) to visualise velocity triangles,
pump and system curves, and the Fanno and Rayleigh lines, so that students explore the
effect of each parameter before solving problems by hand.
\item \emph{Engineering examples from aerodynamics and propulsion} in units 3 and 6, drawn
from my experience in Formula Student aerodynamics (drag-reduction system), industrial
simulation and high-speed flows.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Master's subject: Computational Fluid Mechanics}\label{sec:cfm}
The subject (about 1.5~h of theory + 1.5~h of laboratory per week) covers Python for
scientific computing, numerical methods (ODEs, PDEs, linear algebra), an
incompressible/compressible Navier--Stokes solver, high-performance computing (OpenMP, MPI,
CUDA) and computational flow analysis (stability, POD, DMD). Assessment combines
deliverables, a course project in pairs and a final exam. My research experience relates
directly to the HPC and compressible-flow parts. I propose:
\begin{itemize}
\item a GPU laboratory session that accelerates the subject's Jacobi/Poisson example with
CUDA or OpenACC and measures speed-up and scalability;
\item extending the flow-analysis topic with data-driven and causal analysis of time
series (e.g.\ the lid-driven cavity case), linked to research line B;
\item course projects that can grow into TFM theses within the research plan.
\end{itemize}
